4 days Copilot assessment prompt built, calibrated over 13 runs

Build vs buy, decided in a week

Hundreds of back-office colleagues needed moving into voice roles. First we had to know who could already do it, who was trainable, and who wasn't suited. That meant assessing all of them.

The obvious route was a commercial assessment vendor — roughly ₹4 lakh for the cohort. I tested Microsoft Copilot against a single recorded call instead. It worked.

Then I built for real: 13 live interviews, deliberately starting at both extremes of English fluency and working inward, until the prompt returned a structured score table that held steady no matter who ran it. Four days, zero incremental licence cost. Leadership approved it on the spot.

15 min Briefing that let 20 team leaders run it themselves

Scale through people, not heroics

I couldn't assess several hundred people alone, and a tool only one person can operate isn't a tool — it's a bottleneck.

So I designed it down to four steps: schedule the call, record it in Teams, run the prompt, collect the score. That made the handover a 15-minute briefing to 20 team leaders — no deck, no manual, no support queue.

I split the cohort into 10 groups with two TLs each, and paired it with a Copilot-built tracker and a Power BI dashboard that reached leadership every morning.

370 Colleagues assessed in 10 working days

Idea to full execution at speed

370 assessments in 10 working days — 99% of them inside 8. Not by working longer, but by removing every step that didn't have to be there.

Results fed a five-stage pipeline: triage into voice-ready, trainable and not-suited; a 2.5-day voice and accent course built with L&D; reassessment on the identical rubric; then redeployment.

I split the trainable group by proximity to the pass mark and ran the closer cohort first, so the course was already tuned by the time the group needing most support reached it. 12 batches, ~128 trained, 50% converted, 68% average score lift, 89 people redeployed.

97.83% 362 of 370 scores held up on review

The operators caught what the tool missed

362 of 370 scores held. 8 did not. They surfaced because the team leaders running the assessments flagged them — a weak candidate scored into the top bucket, or a strong one scored below what the interviewer had just watched.

That is the part I would keep in any future build. The operators weren't passive button-pressers; they were the check on the model, and they were close enough to the candidate to know when a number looked wrong. Those 8 were reassessed and corrected.

Cleared the veto in advance

I want to build this assessment engine into a real product. So I went looking for what would stop it — and found the group accessibility policy. Any AI that scores how a person speaks is going to meet an HR objection: that it disadvantages candidates with speech impediments or neurodivergence. Fair objection. It would have killed the pitch.

So I answered it before it was asked. I ran the experiment deliberately: could the evaluator score the capability the job requires without the disability leaking into the result? It could. I now have the case study in hand.

The roadblock is cleared before day one of building. I care about getting this right — assessment that judges people on what they can do is worth building for its own sake. What I would also want noticed is the method: go and find whoever will say no, work out why, and arrive with their answer already evidenced.
